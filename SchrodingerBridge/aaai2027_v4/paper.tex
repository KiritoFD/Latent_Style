\begin{abstract}
Current style transfer methods face two primary challenges. First, state-of-the-art diffusion models, along with training-free methods built on them, require more than 60\,GB of VRAM and therefore cannot run on consumer GPUs. Second, lightweight methods without these generative backbones often underperform a trivial identity mapping, which scores high on traditional metrics like ArtFID and LPIPS but was rarely evaluated as a baseline. 

We empirically observe that frequency dominance in shared representation spaces pushes lightweight models toward identity-like solutions~\cite{rahaman2019spectral}: low-frequency structural energy overwhelms high-frequency style gradients, a pattern we confirm through gradient-spectrum analysis and per-subband style-transfer measurements.

We argue that an effective style transfer method should outperform the identity baseline while remaining computationally affordable. To this end, we propose WEAVE: a single-level Haar wavelet transform splits the VAE latent into a low-frequency structure band and high-frequency style bands; a compact rectified-flow network learns a structure-preserving velocity field; and endpoint statistical alignment (AdaIN) injects the target style into the high-frequency subbands. This decoupling separates content transport from style injection, letting WEAVE train in 3 minutes on a single consumer GPU.

We also introduce the IDT--TGT sandwich protocol, which brackets each result between two intuitive references: IDT returns the source unchanged, while TGT returns an unrelated image from the target style. IDT reveals style suppression; TGT reveals content collapse, as when StyleAligned moves farther from the source than TGT itself (LPIPS 0.869 vs.~0.733). Under this protocol, WEAVE clears the identity reference with only 903K trainable parameters (plus a frozen 84M VAE), making high-quality style transfer both effective and affordable.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Style transfer faces a practical tension. Large diffusion editors can require more than 60\,GB of GPU memory, while lightweight models may preserve the source so strongly that their style score falls below the unchanged input. Metrics such as LPIPS and ArtFID can hide this no-op behavior because they reward source reconstruction and rarely report identity as a baseline.
Figure~\ref{fig:page1} adds two simple references. Identity (IDT) returns the source image, and Target Style (TGT) returns an unrelated reference from the requested style. A useful method should move beyond IDT in style without moving as far from the source as TGT. This bracket exposes both style suppression (e.g., SaMam) and content collapse (e.g., StyleAligned) in the original metric units.

We trace the identity shortcut to frequency dominance rather than model size. Neural networks fit low-frequency signals first~\cite{rahaman2019spectral,xu2022frequency}, and natural images place much more energy in global structure than in fine texture. When flow matching learns both in one coordinate system, structural errors dominate the update and weaken the style-conditioned signal, a conflict also observed in frequency-domain translation~\cite{cai2021fdit}.

To solve the conflict between structure and style, we propose WEAVE.
WEAVE keeps the pre-trained VAE but replaces the diffusion network with a 903K-parameter velocity model. A one-level Haar transform separates low-frequency structure (LL) from high-frequency texture (LH/HL/HH). The flow learns content-preserving transport, and one endpoint AdaIN update injects style into the high-frequency bands. WEAVE trains in 3 minutes on one consumer GPU, and the same latent operation can be added to a frozen SD1.5 pipeline without training (Section~\ref{sec:plugin}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~\textbf{Evaluation:} The IDT--TGT sandwich reports each method between a no-op reference and a target-style reference, revealing style suppression and content collapse.
(2)~\textbf{Method:} WEAVE separates structure and texture with Haar wavelets, then combines compact flow matching with endpoint high-frequency AdaIN.
(3)~\textbf{Efficiency:} WEAVE uses 903K trainable parameters, trains in 3 minutes on one RTX~3060, and transfers its latent operator to frozen SD1.5 without training.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Neural Style Transfer}
\label{sec:rw-nst}
Artistic style transfer was introduced by optimizing pixel spaces to match feature statistics of a pretrained network~\cite{gatys2015neural,gatys2016image}, and was accelerated by matching feature statistics through normalization or transform layers, e.g., AdaIN~\cite{huang2017arbitrary} and whitening--coloring transforms (WCT)~\cite{li2017universal}.
Subsequent GAN- and contrastive-learning approaches address unpaired translation, notably CUT~\cite{park2020cut}; later feed-forward and attention-based variants improve speed and fidelity~\cite{park2019sanet,deng2022stytr2,hong2023aespa,kwon2024aesfa}, while training-free directions reuse pretrained codebooks~\cite{gim2024cast} or hierarchical vision transformers~\cite{zhang2024s2wat}, and diffusion backbones enable prompt-guided transfer~\cite{zhang2024artbank}.
A recent survey frames a decade of progress and open gaps~\cite{zhang2025survey}; however, these methods either need per-style optimization, risk content distortion when content and style share low-frequency statistics, or still demand substantial compute, leaving room for lightweight yet faithful transfer.

\subsection{Diffusion-based and Training-free Style Editing}
\label{sec:rw-diffusion}
Latent diffusion models~\cite{rombach2022high} shifted style transfer from explicit feature transforms to generative editing in compressed latent spaces.
Attention-control and inversion techniques enable training-free editing---Prompt-to-Prompt~\cite{hertz2022prompt} and SDEdit~\cite{meng2021sdedit}---while attention-manipulation methods such as StyleID~\cite{chung2024style} and StyleAligned~\cite{hertz2024style}, image-prompt adapters like IP-Adapter~\cite{ye2023ipadapter}, and inversion-based schemes like InST~\cite{zhang2023inst} and DiffuseIT~\cite{kim2022diffuseit} broaden the toolkit, with ArtBank~\cite{zhang2024artbank} and Z-STAR~\cite{lu2024zstar} as recent training-free examples; yet state-of-the-art backbones (FLUX~\cite{blackforestlabs2024flux}, Qwen-Image~\cite{alibaba2024qwen}, Seedream) and their editing variants rely on 60\,GB+ VRAM and massive pretrained priors, limiting access on consumer hardware and motivating lightweight alternatives.

\subsection{Flow Matching and Schr\"odinger Bridges for Image Translation}
\label{sec:rw-sb}
Transport-based formulations offer a principled alternative to adversarial translation.
Rectified flow~\cite{liu2022rectified} and flow matching~\cite{lipman2022flow} learn straight transport maps with few integration steps, while the Schr\"odinger bridge (SB) problem~\cite{leonard2014survey} and its diffusion/flow solvers---DSB~\cite{debortoli2021dsb}, Diffusion SB Matching (DSBM)~\cite{shi2023dsbm}, I2SB~\cite{liu2023i2sb}, and DDIB~\cite{su2023ddib}---couple transport to optimal transport~\cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn,peyre2019computational} to map between distributions without paired data, but they learn a map in a shared coordinate basis where content and style compete, so they inherit the same low-frequency bias that can drive identity collapse in style transfer, and few exploit frequency structure to separate the two.

\subsection{Lightweight Architectures and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:rw-light}
To reduce cost, recent lightweight designs train small networks on pixel or feature spaces, e.g., Mamba-based SaMST and SaMam~\cite{liu2024samst,liu2024samam,gu2023mamba}, yet standard protocols---LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable} and ArtFID~\cite{wright2022artfid}---heavily reward source reconstruction and historically omit the identity mapping as an explicit baseline, so methods that merely preserve content can appear competitive; this motivates explicitly including the identity mapping as a direction-aware evaluation baseline.

\subsection{Spectral Bias and Frequency-aware Optimization}
\label{sec:rw-spectral}
Neural networks exhibit a well-documented spectral bias~\cite{rahaman2019spectral,xu2022frequency}, preferentially fitting low-frequency signals; together with the $1/f$ spectral distribution of natural images, this motivates frequency-aware optimization.
Frequency-domain translation explicitly constrains low-/high-frequency statistics to protect content identity~\cite{cai2021fdit}.
Prior work applies spectral decomposition to restoration and generation, but the link between spectral bias and the identity shortcut in style transfer has not been drawn; we hypothesize that when content and style share one coordinate basis the optimizer's low-frequency preference suppresses style, motivating a decoupling of the two in the frequency domain.

\subsection{Wavelet and Latent-space Alignment}
\label{sec:rw-wavelet}
Wavelet transforms separate multi-scale information in restoration and generation~\cite{phung2023wavelet} and have been adapted to style transfer~\cite{yoo2019photorealistic}; the Haar basis in particular gives exact orthogonality and additive norm splitting~\cite{daubechies1992ten}.
Concurrently, latent diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} yields regularized latent spaces where statistical alignment such as WCT~\cite{li2017universal} is stable.
\paragraph{Motivation.}
Combining wavelet decomposition with latent-space statistical alignment inside a flow-matching transport---structurally separating content from style rather than competing in a shared space---directly addresses the limitations above and motivates the WEAVE framework in Section~\ref{sec:method}.



\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

WEAVE learns a compact transport in VAE latent space, separates structure from texture with a Haar transform, and injects target statistics once at the endpoint.



\subsection{Framework Setup}
\label{sec:framework}

Let $z_0=\mathcal E(x_c)\in\mathbb R^{4\times32\times32}$ be the SD1.5 VAE latent of a content image and let $z_1\sim p_s$ be a training image from target style $s$~\cite{rombach2022high}. Rectified flow uses
\begin{equation}
z_t = (1-t)z_0 + tz_1,\quad u_t = z_1 - z_0,\quad t \sim \mathcal U([0,1]),
\end{equation}
and learns $u_t$ with eight Euler steps at inference.

\subsection{Wavelet Decomposition and Frequency Routing}
\label{sec:wavelet}

Low-frequency structure carries much more energy than fine texture, so a shared loss favors source reconstruction~\cite{rahaman2019spectral,xu2022frequency,cai2021fdit}. WEAVE separates these signals before predicting the flow.

\paragraph{Haar wavelet decomposition.}
A one-level Haar transform gives $\mathcal W(z_t)=(\ell_t,h_{1,t},h_{2,t},h_{3,t})$: one LL structure band and three high-frequency bands, each $4{\times}16{\times}16$. Haar is orthogonal and local, so high-frequency updates do not directly alter the LL coordinate~\cite{daubechies1992ten}.

\paragraph{Empirical frequency probes.}
Across 5,000 cross-style latent pairs, transport energy concentrates in LL while style separation is strongest in HH (Figure~\ref{fig:frequency-probes}). This motivates a weak LL loss and stronger high-frequency routing.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
The 903K-parameter velocity network has four width-64 residual blocks and separate $1{\times}1$ heads for LL, LH, and HL. HH has no velocity head and changes only through endpoint AdaIN. LL bypasses style attention, and RMSNorm retains the channel means used for tone. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows the full path.

\paragraph{Spectral flow-matching objective.}
The spectral flow-matching term is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{impl}}:=\mathbb E_t\big[&
\lambda_{LL}\|v_\ell-u_\ell\|_2^2
+\|v_{h_1}-u_{h_1}\|_2^2\\
&+\|v_{h_2}-u_{h_2}\|_2^2\big],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:weave-loss}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{LL}=0.3$. The lower LL weight limits structural dominance while still allowing coarse tone to move. The predicted endpoint is $\hat z_1=z_0+\mathcal W^{-1}(v_\ell,v_{h_1},v_{h_2},0)$.

\paragraph{Mild LL partial stylization.}
The LL training target blends the source LL with 30\% AdaIN-aligned target LL. This small shift carries coarse color; stronger blends damage content without improving DINO-S (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Endpoint AdaIN: Primary Style Injection Channel}
\label{sec:eota}

Flow matching alone carries a weak style signal. WEAVE therefore applies AdaIN once, after the last Euler step, to align high-frequency statistics without repeatedly disturbing the transport path. Removing this endpoint update largely returns the model to source preservation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Alignment operator.}
Let $\hat h_i$ and $h_i^\star$ be high-frequency subbands of the predicted latent and a target-style reference. For $i\in\{1,2,3\}$, AdaIN~\cite{huang2017arbitrary} gives
\begin{equation}
T_i(a) = \frac{a-\hat\mu_i}{\hat\sigma_i}\,\sigma_i^\star+\mu_i^\star,
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are channel-wise spatial statistics. We blend this update with scales $(s_1,s_2,s_3)=(0.3,0.3,0.5)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat h_i^+ = (1-s_i)\hat h_i + s_i\,T_i(\hat h_i),\qquad i\in\{1,2,3\},
\end{equation}
LL is left unchanged in this endpoint operation.


\subsection{Training and Inference}

Training adds an edge $\ell_1$ loss (weight 0.1) and a low-pass MSE anchor to Eq.~\ref{eq:weave-loss}; together they contribute less than 5\% of the gradient. Full hyperparameters are in the supplement. At inference, we use eight Euler steps and apply high-frequency AdaIN only after the final step.

\subsection{Setup and Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

Distinct5-WikiArt-512 (D5) contains five deliberately dissimilar styles, with 3{,}600 training and 150 test images per style. We evaluate 750 ordered pairs and also test P2A-256 and all 20 WikiArt families (R5; 12{,}000 pairs). DINO-S is the primary style metric and CLIP-S is secondary; LPIPS and DINO-C measure source preservation.

For each D5 request, IDT copies the source and TGT returns a fixed, unrelated image from the target style. The desired region is above IDT on style and better than TGT on content. These are empirical reference endpoints, not hard bounds; full construction and metric definitions are in the supplement. Table~\ref{tab:main} compares ten baselines. Learned models use a held-out 50-pair validation split, and evaluation uses fixed seeds.

\paragraph{Reading the sandwich.}
On D5, SaMST and SaMam score below IDT on CLIP-S, so their content scores alone do not establish transfer. At the other extreme, StyleAligned has higher LPIPS than TGT (0.869 vs.~0.733) and nearly reaches the TGT DINO-C value (0.239 vs.~0.176). The two references expose opposite failures without changing the underlying metrics.

\paragraph{Main comparison.}
WEAVE clears IDT on both style metrics while retaining substantially more content than methods with similar or higher style scores. Among trained methods above the IDT CLIP-S reference, it has the lowest D5 LPIPS and is the only sub-million-parameter model. It also clears IDT on P2A and R5, where the style space expands to 20 families. Figure~\ref{fig:radar} summarizes the same trade-off across all metrics.

WEAVE trains in 3 minutes on one RTX~3060 and generates 750 images in 95\,s. Latent-WCT is faster but falls below IDT, showing that the analytic wavelet transform alone does not replace learned transport.

\subsection{Plug-and-Play Extension to Pre-trained Diffusion Models}
\label{sec:plugin}

We add the same Haar and endpoint AdaIN operation to frozen SD1.5 image-to-image output (20-step DDIM) without training. On 600 off-diagonal D5 pairs, it raises CLIP-S and DINO-S ($p{<}10^{-15}$, paired $t$-test) with little content change. The operation adds 4.9\,ms per image.


\subsection{Mechanism Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

At $t{=}0.5$, the unweighted LL loss is $5{\times}$ larger than the high-frequency losses and its head gradient is $6{\times}$ larger. LL remains the largest input-gradient band after down-weighting. Output probes then show that LH/HL carry most style change, LL retains structure, and headless HH changes only through endpoint AdaIN. These measurements support frequency dominance and the intended division of labor.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The evidence supports a simple interpretation: flow matching moves the latent while preserving source structure, and endpoint AdaIN supplies the high-frequency style signal that the flow loss underweights. Neither component is sufficient alone (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
WEAVE is domain-conditional rather than reference-image conditional. D5 contains only five styles, partly addressed by R5 and Other5. Protecting LL also makes palette-dominated styles harder, and one Haar level may miss style cues that span several scales.

\section{Conclusion}

The IDT--TGT sandwich makes two failures visible: a model may stay too close to the source or discard more content than a target-style reference. WEAVE avoids both by separating low-frequency structure from high-frequency style. Its 903K-parameter network trains in 3 minutes on one RTX~3060, and the same endpoint operation improves frozen SD1.5 without training.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Style transfer tools can be misused to produce deceptive images or to misattribute stylistic authorship.
Our method operates at the domain level (e.g., ``Impressionism'') rather than the per-artist level.
This reduces the risk of imitating a specific living artist.
We do not condition on individual reference paintings.
The dataset is sourced from WikiArt and contains historically significant artworks.
It does not contain personal data.
We encourage downstream users to disclose when an image has been style-transferred and to respect source-content licensing terms.

All experiments use public data (WikiArt) and a public VAE (Stable Diffusion v1.5 EMA VAE).
The experiments section gives the training configuration, hyperparameters, and evaluation protocol.
Training takes 3 minutes on a single RTX 3060 (12\,GB; 10-epoch wall-clock, batch 96).
Generation-only inference for 750 pairs needs about 95 seconds (8-step ODE + VAE decode; $\approx$71\,ms network generation and $\approx$52\,ms decode per image), and a full metrics pass (CLIP/LPIPS on top of generation) is about 2 minutes.
The 903K trainable parameter backbone checkpoint is 3.5\,MB.
We will release the code, the checkpoint, and the 750-pair evaluation protocol upon publication.
